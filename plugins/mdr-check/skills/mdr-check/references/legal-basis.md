# Legal basis

Pointers for the review and for the lawyer who decides. Not a legal assessment. Verify anything load-bearing against the current consolidated text.

## Why marketing copy is the problem

MDR Art. 2(12) defines intended purpose as the use for which a device is intended according to the data supplied by the manufacturer on the label, in the instructions for use **or in promotional or sales materials or statements**, and as specified in the clinical evaluation.

That is the whole reason this check exists. A company can hold internal documentation saying "lifestyle app, no medical purpose" and still establish a medical intended purpose through a headline. The marketing text is not a description of the regulatory status — it is one of the sources that sets it.

Consequence: the qualification question is decided on what the copy promises, not on what the product was meant to be.

## MDR — Regulation (EU) 2017/745

**Art. 2(1) — definition.** A medical device is an instrument, apparatus, appliance, software, implant, reagent, material or other article intended by the manufacturer for human beings for one or more of these medical purposes:

- diagnosis, prevention, monitoring, prediction, prognosis, treatment or alleviation of disease
- diagnosis, monitoring, treatment, alleviation of, or compensation for, an injury or disability
- investigation, replacement or modification of the anatomy or of a physiological or pathological process or state
- providing information by means of in vitro examination of specimens derived from the human body

and which does not achieve its principal intended action by pharmacological, immunological or metabolic means. Also covered: devices for the control or support of conception, and products for cleaning, disinfecting or sterilising devices.

Use this list as the Step 3 checklist. Each bullet is a purpose a headline can accidentally claim.

**Recital 19 — the safe harbour.** Software specifically intended by the manufacturer for one or more of the medical purposes in the definition is a medical device. Software for general purposes, even when used in a healthcare setting, and software intended for lifestyle and well-being purposes, is not a medical device. This is the positioning the copy has to stay inside.

**Art. 5(1) — market access.** A device may only be placed on the market or put into service if it complies with the Regulation, which includes conformity assessment and CE marking. Copy that establishes a medical intended purpose for a product that never went through conformity assessment turns an uncertified product into an unlawfully marketed device.

**Art. 7 — prohibition on misleading claims.** In labelling, instructions for use, making available, putting into service and advertising, it is prohibited to use text, names, trade marks, pictures and figurative or other signs that may mislead the user or patient about intended purpose, safety or performance by:

- (a) ascribing functions and properties the device does not have
- (b) creating a false impression regarding treatment or diagnosis, functions or properties the device does not have
- (c) failing to inform of a likely risk associated with use in line with the intended purpose
- (d) suggesting uses other than those stated to form part of the intended purpose for which conformity assessment was carried out

Art. 7 is the article that matters for a product that **is** CE-marked. Then the exposure is not "accidentally a device" but "claiming beyond the certified Zweckbestimmung", which is (d).

**Annex VIII Rule 11** classifies software that provides information used for decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. Relevant when the copy describes exactly that function.

**Annex XVI** lists product groups without an intended medical purpose that are nonetheless regulated (including certain contact lenses, dermal fillers, equipment for lipolysis, high-intensity electromagnetic and brain-stimulation equipment). Aesthetic products cannot escape the MDR by dropping medical wording.

**Guidance.** MDCG 2019-11 on qualification and classification of software under MDR and IVDR is the working document for apps and standalone software; MDCG 2020-1 covers clinical evaluation of medical device software. Both are guidance, not binding law, and both are what an authority will reach for.

## Germany

**MPDG.** The Medizinprodukterecht-Durchführungsgesetz implements the MDR nationally, in force since 26 May 2021. Market surveillance sits with the Länder authorities; BfArM and PEI hold federal roles. The act carries criminal and administrative-fine provisions (§§ 92 ff.), including for placing devices on the market without the required conformity assessment and for misleading designations. Treat the exact provision as a question for counsel.

**HWG — Heilmittelwerbegesetz.** Two entry doors matter:

- § 1 Abs. 1 Nr. 1a brings Medizinprodukte into scope.
- § 1 Abs. 1 Nr. 2 brings **other** means, procedures, treatments and objects into scope *as far as the advertising statement refers to detecting, removing or alleviating diseases, ailments, bodily injuries or pathological complaints*.

The second one is the trap people miss: a product that is not a medical device still falls under the HWG the moment its advertising makes a disease claim. Dropping the device status does not drop the advertising rules.

- § 3 prohibits misleading advertising, expressly including ascribing effects the product does not have and creating the expectation of success that cannot be expected with certainty.
- § 11 Abs. 1 restricts advertising to the general public, thematically: technical or foreign-language terminology, statements that the product is recommended or examined by healthcare professionals, case histories, depictions of persons in professional clothing, third-party statements and letters of thanks, fear-based advertising, and advertising aimed at children. The numbering was reworked in 2012 — check the current text before citing a specific number.
- § 3a is medicine-specific (unauthorised medicinal products). Do not apply it to devices; the device analogue is MDR Art. 5 read together with HWG § 3.

**UWG — Gesetz gegen den unlauteren Wettbewerb.** This is the realistic litigation path, and the one the company should worry about first:

- § 5 misleading commercial practice, § 5a misleading by omission
- § 3a Rechtsbruch — breaching a market-conduct rule such as MDR Art. 5 or the HWG is itself an unfair practice
- § 8 claims for injunctive relief

Competitors, industry associations and the Wettbewerbszentrale can send an Abmahnung and obtain an injunction, with costs, contractual penalties and a takedown, without any authority being involved. It is faster and far more common than a regulatory proceeding. An MDR breach does not need an MDR enforcement action to hurt.

## Adjacent regimes to flag, not to assess here

- **Food and supplements.** Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006 on nutrition and health claims, plus the ban on disease-related claims for food (§ 12 LFGB). A supplement making a disease claim has a food-law problem on top of the HWG one.
- **Cosmetics.** Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009. A cosmetic claiming a therapeutic effect is misclassified.
- **Medicinal products.** Directive 2001/83/EC and the AMG. Presentation as preventing or treating disease can qualify a product as a medicinal product by presentation, which is the strictest outcome of all.
- **Data protection.** Health data is Art. 9 GDPR special-category data. Out of scope here; note it when copy promises processing of symptom data.
- **Professional advertising rules.** Berufsordnungen of the Landesärztekammern apply when clinicians appear as endorsers.

## The other direction

Sometimes the medical device status is the goal: DiGA reimbursement under § 33a SGB V requires a CE-marked Class I or IIa medical device. When that is the plan, the answer is not to strip the claims but to sequence them — market inside the lifestyle scope until conformity assessment is done, then market the certified Zweckbestimmung. Say that instead of pushing rewrites that undercut the roadmap.
